## Forehead
- Anatomy/boundaries: Frontalis origin galea aponeurotica, insertion orbicularis oculi + procerus, no bony attachment; 88% of individuals show frontalis bifurcation, 46% of those 88% have microscopic muscle fibers at/beyond the bifurcation (Table 6.1, DermUS p.104). Wrinkle: horizontal forehead lines. Deep fat: "Lateral forehead fat pad" and "Glabelar fat pad" drawn in Fig. 6.2b (p.102).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book) — only generic face dermis ~1.0–1.5 mm (see General).
- Injection plane & technique: (no data in this book)
- Volumes: (no data in this book)
- Product/rheology: (no data in this book)
- Expected surface response & longevity: (no data in this book)
- Danger zones: "Temple and Lateral Part of the Frontal Region — main risk is injury of the frontal and parietal branches of the temporal artery" (DermUS p.127). "Parietotemporal Region — parietal branch of the temporal artery runs in the subaponeurotic layer" (p.127). Supraorbital artery found by looking for its notch in the bony margin ("A tip for finding some of the arteries is to look for the corresponding notch in the bony margin", p.119). Case: granulomatous reaction after mesotherapy in frontal region, granulomatous tissue in dermis at 24 MHz and dermis+hypodermis at 70 MHz (Fig. 22.9, p.424).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Glabella
- Anatomy/boundaries: Corrugator origin — medial supraorbital rim 46%, medial frontal bone 31%, medial infraorbital rim 17%, upper nasal process 7%; insertion medial half of brow skin; pulls medial eyebrows together → vertical glabellar/frown lines. Procerus: fascia on nasal bones → fibrofatty glabellar tissue/frontal fascia, pyramid-shaped → horizontal/bunny lines (Table 6.1, p.104). Glabellar fat pad = deep fat (Fig. 6.2b, p.102).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book)
- Injection plane & technique: (no data in this book)
- Volumes: (no data in this book) — but one real deposit size: silicone-oil deposit in the glabellar region measured 2.5 cm (transverse) × 0.8 cm (thickness) × 3.2 cm (longitudinal) in a 56-y/o female (Reporting Cosmetic Fillers, DermUS p.494).
- Product/rheology: (no data in this book)
- Expected surface response & longevity: (no data in this book)
- Danger zones: "Glabellar Region — presents the HIGHEST rate of major complications, including blindness, because it gathers important and interconnected arteries subject to injury: supratrochlear, supraorbital, and dorsal nasal arteries" (DermUS p.127). Mechanism of blindness: retrograde flow from filler injected into supraorbital/supratrochlear artery, then anterograde into central retinal artery → occlusion (p.418). Brain infarction via communications of dorsal nasal, anterior/posterior ethmoidal and ophthalmic arteries (p.418). Occlusion typically evident in first 24–48 h (p.418). Ref. for US-guided safe glabellar filling: Lee W et al. Aesthet Surg J 2020 (ref 26, ch.27 p.524); artery localisation on central forehead: Phumyoo et al. Clin Anat 2020;33:370–82 (ref 29, p.524).
- Case: active morphea after HA + CaHA fillers with glabellar-region dermal hypoechogenicity and hypodermal hyperechogenicity at 24 MHz (Fig. 22.6, p.422).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Brow
- Anatomy: Retro-orbicularis oculi fat (ROOF) is a deep fat pad (Fig. 6.2b, p.102). Dermoid cysts "can generate a bump in the tail of the eyebrow" (CME pediatric Q7, p.545). Eyebrows appear as monolaminar hyperechoic structures (p.92).
- Everything else: (no data in this book)

## Temple
- Anatomy: "Lateral temporal fat" = deep fat pad (Fig. 6.2b, p.102). Temporal artery on grayscale/color Doppler = anechoic tubular structure (Fig. 5.20, p.97). Superficial temporal artery has four main branches: transverse facial, frontal, middle temporal, parietal (p.111).
- Layer thickness / volumes / plane: (no data in this book). Reference cited for US-guided temple lipofilling: Kadouch, Schelke, Swift. Aesthet Surg J 2021;41(5):603–12 (ref 38, p.431).
- Danger zones: frontal & parietal branches of temporal artery (p.127). Preauricular region: "main risk is damage of the temporal vessels, both artery and vein; facial and trigeminal nerves are difficult to identify on ultrasound" (p.127).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Upper eyelid & periorbital
- Anatomy/boundaries: eyelid = 3 layers: anterior (epidermis+dermis, hair follicles more in upper lid), middle = orbicularis oculi, posterior = tarsal plate with Meibomian glands (p.103). "Hypodermal fatty tissue is ABSENT in the eyelids and lips" (Ch.5 p.90) and the "lack of superficial periorbicular fat pad" is drawn in Fig. 6.2a (p.102). Orbicularis oculi has 3 parts: orbital (ellipse, outer, voluntary closure), palpebral (upper+lower lids, involuntary closure/reflex blink), lacrimal (tensor tarsi); antagonist levator palpebrae superioris; wrinkles = crow's feet, "tear throughs", nasojugal groove (Table 6.1, p.104). Orbital fat pads drawn: upper lid preaponeurotic, nasal, central fat pads, lacrimal gland, temporal fat pad, interpad septum (arcuate expansion of Lockwood's ligament); lower lid: orbital fat, tear trough, arcus, SOOF (Fig. 6.16, p.114). Eyelid vascularity: anastomoses of ophthalmic branches (supraorbital, supratrochlear, dorsal nasal) + lacrimal artery with external carotid branches (facial, angular, superficial temporal, infraorbital); of superficial temporal branches, transverse facial and frontal contribute significantly (pp.106–111).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book)
- Injection plane & technique: Ch.28 warning — "high G-prime substances are used under thin skin, such as the lower eyelids" is a cause of problems; cannulas starting deep to orbicularis oculi laterally can penetrate the muscle medially, creating an opening for filler to move superficial to the muscle (DermUS p.525).
- Volumes: (no data in this book)
- Expected surface response: fat grafts in orbicularis oculi of eyelid are hypoechoic oval nodules, hypovascular, distort anatomy (Fig. 22.12, p.426).
- Danger zones: lacrimal gland inflammation (ASIA-type reaction) reported with all filler types; glands stay normal size, no duct dilation, mild hypervascularity (p.422–423). Blepharoplasty late changes: thinning of orbicularis muscle, granulomas (p.429).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Tear trough & infraorbital
- Anatomy: SOOF (suborbicularis oculi fat) = deep fat; "Infraorbital or malar fat pad" = superficial fat (Fig. 6.2, p.102). Tear trough and arcus (marginalis) drawn in lower-lid diagram (Fig. 6.16c, p.114). Corrugator partly originates from medial infraorbital rim (17%) (p.104).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book)
- Injection plane & technique: see Upper eyelid — cannula-through-orbicularis migration mechanism (p.525).
- Danger zones: "Infraorbital Region — contains the infraorbital artery that runs close to the bony margin" (p.127). Infraorbital foramen: "patients with severe pain after compression of the infraorbital [nerve] at the infraorbital foramen … resolved instantaneously after ultrasound-guided hyaluronidase injection" (p.528). "Other delicate structures are the various foramina" (p.528).
- Volumes / product / longevity / variation: (no data in this book)

## Midface: malar & medial cheek (deep/superficial fat compartments)
- Anatomy/boundaries: Superficial fat pads drawn (Fig. 6.2a, p.102): lateral cheek fat pad, medial cheek fat pad, infraorbital/malar fat pad, nasolabial fat pad. Deep fat pads (Fig. 6.2b): SOOF, buccal fat pad (body + lower part). Zygomaticus major: zygomatic bone → modiolus; 34% show a bifid structure lateral to zygomaticus minor; wrinkles = nasolabial lines, midcheek lines/furrows (p.104). Zygomaticus minor inserts into fibrofatty hypodermal tissue (p.104). Levator labii superioris (deeper) and LLS alaeque nasi (superficial) both visible on US (Fig. 6.7, p.108).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book) except normal cheek skin at 70 MHz shows tiny dermal hair follicles (Fig. 5.4, p.91).
- Injection plane & technique: Ch.28: "High G-prime HA fillers placed on the periost of the zygoma or chin are oval pockets. That is also the case in superficial and deep fat pads. In deep fat pads, where movements because of muscular activity are minimal, the filler seems to stay unaltered for many months" (DermUS p.526). "After HA filler injection on the bone deep to the masseter muscle, we observed HA fillers not confined to an oval pocket but as a meandering anechoic structure between different muscle strains" (p.526).
- Volumes: (no data in this book)
- Expected surface response & longevity: deep-fat-pad HA "stays unaltered for many months" (p.526). Abscess case in right malar region (Fig. 27.1, p.518).
- Migration: "The most common complication of polyalkylimide and polyacrylamide hydrogels is implant migration, which usually occurs after years of treatment. The most common is CAUDAL migration, especially in MALAR implants, which eventually form lumps, sometimes large, at the corners of the mouth" (Ch.25, DermUS p.504).
- Danger zones: accessory parotid glands / prominent anterior prolongation of parotid lie on top of the upper third of the masseter along the parotid duct axis and can be injured (p.119, Fig. 6.29 p.121). "Filler injections in the parotid gland are frequently seen. The subcutaneous fat pad in that area, being the targeted area, is notoriously thin. Measuring this space using ultrasound can help the physician's technique" (p.528). Transverse facial artery (Fig. 6.30, p.122).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Lateral cheek & zygomatic arch
- Anatomy: lateral cheek fat pad (superficial), masseter origin on zygomatic arch + maxillary process of zygomatic bone (Table 6.1, p.105). Risorius origin parotid/masseter fascia and platysma, thin bundle that may partially cover masseter (p.105).
- Danger zones: transverse facial artery; parotid gland/accessory glands (see Midface).
- Everything else: (no data in this book)

## Preauricular
- Danger zones: "main risk is the damage of the temporal vessels, both the artery and the vein. The main facial and trigeminal nerves are difficult to identify on ultrasound" (DermUS p.127). Parotid arterial supply: posterior auricular + superficial temporal arteries (p.97).
- Everything else: (no data in this book)

## Nose
- Anatomy/boundaries: three US zones. Hypodermis is NOT prominent fatty lobules but a fibroadipose layer; dermis thicker at the tip and slightly more hypoechoic than proximal/middle parts (p.113). Proximal: paired procerus (hypoechoic longitudinal bands) over convex hyperechoic nasal bones. Dorsum: nasalis (transverse part) very thin, almost imperceptible; beneath it the two convex hypoechoic upper lateral cartilages. Tip: alar (lower lateral) cartilages as two convex hypoechoic bands; columella = lower alar cartilages + mostly fibrous tissue (pp.113–115, Figs. 6.17–6.21). Nasalis alar part = dilator naris posterior (p.115). Sebaceous glands prominent in nasal skin (Fig. 5.8, p.92).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book)
- Injection plane & technique: rhinomodulation / filler rhinoplasty mentioned (p.429); ref. Lee W et al. Nasal dorsum augmentation using soft tissue filler injection, J Cosmet Dermatol 2019 (ref 33, p.530). "After nose surgery, one should be very careful" re arterial variants (p.528).
- Volumes: one real deposit: HA overfilling of the nasal tip = 1.93 cm long deposit in the hypodermis (Fig. 22.3, DermUS p.420).
- Expected surface response: overfilling → palpable bump (p.419–420). Rhinoplasty complication: dermal extrusion of cartilage implant 4 months post-op (Fig. 22.17, p.430).
- Danger zones: nasal vascularity from facial-artery branches (angular, alar, dorsal nasal) plus supraorbital/infraorbital branches to the upper nose (p.116). Dorsal nasal artery is part of the glabellar blindness circuit (p.127). Alar artery visible on power Doppler (Fig. 6.36, p.125).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Nasolabial fold
- Anatomy: "Nasolabial fat pad" = superficial fat (Fig. 6.2a, p.102). Zygomaticus major/minor, LLS, LLSAN all produce nasolabial lines (Table 6.1, p.104). LLSAN "Elvis muscle" attached to medial maxilla (p.105).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book)
- Injection plane & technique: ref. Lee W et al., "A Safe Doppler Ultrasound-Guided Method for Nasolabial Fold Correction With Hyaluronic Acid Filler", Aesthet Surg J 2021;41(6):NP486 (ref 25, p.524).
- Volumes: real deposits: silicone oil, right nasofold line 3.2 cm (transverse) × 0.9 cm (thickness) × 4.2 cm (longitudinal); left nasofold line 3.5 × 0.8 × 4.5 cm (Reporting Cosmetic Fillers, DermUS p.494).
- Expected surface response: abscess in right nasolabial fold (Fig. 27.1c, p.518); HA + CaHA both in hypodermis of right nasofold line at 46 MHz (Fig. 22.6b, p.422).
- Danger zones: "Nasofold Region — angular artery, a branch/continuation of the facial artery, receives its name after the alar nasal arteries exit; follows its path through the nasofold lines" (DermUS p.127). Facial artery variants I–III drawn (Fig. 6.31, p.122).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

## Lips & perioral
- Anatomy/boundaries: epidermis, dermis, orbicularis oris (hypoechoic band between cutaneous and submucosal layers), submucosa with minor salivary glands (round/oval hypoechoic) (p.116, Fig. 6.28 p.121). "Hypodermal fatty tissue is absent in the eyelids and lips" (p.90); "lack of superficial perioral fat pad" (Fig. 6.2a, p.102). Lower-lip diagram: skin, vermilion border, mucosa, orbicularis oris, inferior labial artery, horizontal labial artery, vertical labial artery, depressor labii inferioris (Fig. 6.22b, p.118). Modiolus = junction of zygomaticus major, buccinator, platysma pars modiolaris, pars marginalis orbicularis oris, levator anguli oris, mentalis, DLI, DAO, risorius (Fig. 6.8, p.109).
- Layer thickness data: (no data in this book) — deposit "thickness" only (below).
- Injection plane & technique: (no data in this book)
- Volumes: real deposits: silicone oil in upper lip 2.5 cm (longitudinal) × 0.5 cm (thickness); lower lip 2.5 cm × 0.5 cm; deposits "extend to the orbicularis muscle and are continuously found in both lips' lateral and central regions" (Reporting Cosmetic Fillers, DermUS p.494).
- Expected surface response & longevity: HA upper-lip ischemia case — thickening + hypoechogenicity + loss of definition of orbicularis oris borders; reactive dermal/hypodermal hypervascularity from occluded small hypodermal vessels; labial artery itself had normal flow; recovery 48 h after hyaluronidase (Fig. 22.2, pp.418–419). Fat grafts may sit within orbicularis oris (p.425).
- Danger zones: superior/inferior labial arteries (branches of facial artery) "run in the hypodermis of the outer borders and then go deeper to the orbicularis oris muscle" (p.116). Variant: SUPERFICIAL LOOP of the facial artery in the peribuccal region "where this vessel goes towards the skin, usually to the upper hypodermis or to the dermis–hypodermis border" (Fig. 6.40, p.126). Variant: CALIBER-PERSISTENT labial artery — branch that does not taper on entering cutaneous layers; abnormally thick artery in the dermal layer of the lower lip (Fig. 6.41, p.127). Ref: Cotofana labial artery distribution PRS 2017;139:1075 (ref 27, p.128); Tansatit cadaveric lip injections Aesthet Plast Surg 2017;41:430 (ref 44, p.129).
- Mobility: lips are a mobile region; Ch.28 notes fillers stay unaltered in deep fat pads "where movements because of muscular activity are minimal" — implicit contrast with muscular/mobile regions (p.526).
- Variation: (no data in this book)

"Lip mandibular fat pad" and "mandibular fat pad" superficial (Fig. 6.2a).
- Migration target: caudal migration of malar PAAG/polyalkylimide "form lumps … at the corners of the mouth" (Ch.25, p.504).
- Everything else: (no data in this book)

## Jawline & prejowl
- Anatomy: mandibular fat pad, lip-mandibular fat pad, pre-platysmal fat pad (Fig. 6.2a, p.102). Platysma: thin band, overlaps SCM, origin infraclavicular/acromial fibrofatty tissue → anterior/lateral mandible + chin fibrofatty tissue (Table 6.1, p.105).

Filler can migrate to lymph nodes; protocol must include submandibular regions and lacrimal/parotid/submandibular glands (Ch.1 p.11–12, "Protocol for Patients with History of Aesthetic Procedures of the Face").

## General & cross-region

### Layer-stack model (US appearance) — Ch.5 pp.89–90
| Layer | US appearance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Epidermis | hyperechoic line (bilaminar in palms/soles) | echogenicity from stratum corneum keratin; at 46–70 MHz a thin intense hypoechoic band beneath it = non-corneum layers |
| Dermis | hyperechoic band (collagen) | "thinner in the face and ventral forearm, thicker in dorsal and lumbar regions" (p.90); facial dermis "usually measures around 1.0–1.5 mm" (Ch.22, p.417) |
| SLEB | hypoechoic diffuse band in upper dermis | photoaging/elastosis (GAG deposits) in sun-exposed skin — face, neck, forearm dorsum; do not confuse with inflammation (p.89). SLEB thickness decrease used to monitor phototherapy (Ch.14, p.289) |
| Hypodermis | hypoechoic band with hyperechoic linear/wavy fibrous septa | ABSENT in eyelids, lips, proximal nail fold (p.90); nose has fibroadipose layer, not lobules (p.113) |
| Muscles (facial) | hypoechoic band-like structures "of variable thicknesses" | must find axis of muscle to scan (p.103) |
| Cartilage | hypoechoic well-defined wavy band, avascular | nasal alar, ear (Fig. 5.19, p.97) |
| Bone | hyperechoic line with posterior acoustic shadowing | (p.98) |
| Vessels | anechoic tubular; veins compressible, monophasic | intima only visible at 50–70 MHz (p.96) |

- Dermal vessels: at 15–46 MHz machines detect subcutaneous but rarely dermal vessels; at 70 MHz lower dermal (reticular) vessels visible in some areas, velocity ≤15 cm/s (p.90). Normal dermis usually has no detectable color Doppler signal (machine threshold commonly 2 cm/s) (Ch.1, p.7).
- Doppler velocity classes (Ch.1, p.7): Low ≤15 cm/s; Medium 15–35 cm/s; High ≥36 cm/s. Normal skin = low velocity. Report maximum peak systolic velocity; ≥3 samples nonvascular, ≥6 samples vascular lesions.
- Normal joint fluid 1–2 mm laminar is physiological (p.95). Eyelashes/eyebrows monolaminar hyperechoic; scalp hair trilaminar (cuticle-cortex + medulla), body "villus" hair bilaminar (p.91).

### Why "dermal filler" is a misnomer (Ch.22, p.416–417)
- "A common ultrasound finding is that deposits usually land in the HYPODERMIS (subcutaneous tissue) and not the dermis … Therefore, the name 'dermal fillers' is incorrect. This situation is usually due to the thin dermis of the face that usually measures around 1.0–1.5 mm and the length of the needles that come with the kits of around 1.0–1.5 cm" (DermUS p.417).
- "Usually the most involved layer is the hypodermis; however, filler deposits may be found in the dermis and muscular layers, and lymph nodes" (p.418).
- Ch.25 (p.504) contradicts slightly: "When injected for cosmetic purposes, they are expected to be found in the dermis" — treat Ch.22/28 (Wortsman/Schelke) as authoritative.
- Ch.28 (p.525): main cause of problems = injector technique — "too much material is injected or it is delivered too superficial"; "high G-prime substances used under thin skin, such as the lower eyelids".

### Filler identification table (Ch.22 pp.416–417, Fig. 22.1; corrected p.C1)
| Filler | Echo class | US signature | Size over time / persistence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyaluronic acid (HA) | anechoic | oval/round anechoic pseudocystic deposits; lidocaine mixes may have floating echoes; posterior enhancement (bandlike hyperechoic area at deep border, Ch.28 p.526) | decrease in size over months; detectable for several years; cross-linked HA "up to 7 years"; non-cross-linked "commonly lasts around 6 months" (p.417). | Polyacrylamide gel (PAAG) | anechoic | oval anechoic pseudocystic | "do not change in size for at least 18 months" (p.417); semidegradable hydrophilic |

| Polycaprolactone (PLC/PCL) | hypoechoic/heterogeneous | hypoechoic deposits with multiple hyperechoic spots + mini-comet-tail | hypoechoic hydrophilic matrix shrinks over time, hyperechoic spots remain (p.417) |
| Silicone oil (PDMS) | hyperechoic | hyperechoic with diffuse posterior reverberance = "snowstorm" | NO size change; MIGRATES within layers and body regions with movement and gravity (gluteal → calves) (p.417) |

- Ultrasound "has been the best method for detecting and identifying cosmetic fillers due to its high axial spatial resolution compared to MRI or CT" (p.416). Multiple filler types in the same region are common and complicate identification (p.416).

### Filler geometry / spread behaviour (Ch.28 pp.526)
- Immediately post-injection HA = "well-defined homogeneous anechoic pseudocystic pocket". Shape (oval vs longitudinal) depends on G-prime and layer.
- "Since the HA gel itself is very moldable, the filler may spread along the areas where the least resistance is met."
- On bone deep to masseter → meandering between muscle strains. On periosteum of zygoma or chin (high G') → oval pockets. Superficial and deep fat pads → oval pockets; deep fat pads (minimal muscular movement) → unaltered for many months.
- Cannula technique → "several pockets with HA fillers close to each other, connected by pseudoducts."
- Migration mechanisms: intramuscular injection → migration/nodules; cannula punctures through fascia/muscle (lower eyelid) → filler moves superficial to muscle (p.525).
- Silicone oil migrates by gravity/dynamics across regions (p.417); PAAG/polyalkylimide migrate caudally after years, malar → oral commissure (p.504).
- Fillers can migrate to adjacent tissues or LYMPH NODES (Ch.1 protocol p.11; Ch.24 case with submandibular nodes 0.8 & 1.2 cm, p.494).

### Complication timeline (Ch.22 p.418–422; Ch.27 p.517)
- Early ≤4 months: immediate (first 24–48 h) = occlusion (blindness, brain infarction, cyanosis/livedo/necrosis); intermediate (48 h–4 months) = edema (fat inflammation, lymphatic congestion, hydrophilic water retention), overfilling lumps.
- Late >4 months = most frequent; chronic inflammation (dermis hypoechoic, hypodermis hyperechoic, slow-flow hypervascularity), granulomatous reaction (hypoechoic bands/nodules/pseudocapsule around deposit), sarcoid reaction (extends beyond injection sites into old scars), panniculitis (lobular or mixed), morphea-like reaction, lumps/bumps, gland inflammation (lacrimal/parotid/submandibular; ASIA syndrome; xerophthalmia/xerostomia).
- Ch.27 uses a 30-day cut-off: recent (≤30 days) vs late (>30 days) (p.517).
- Arterial occlusion on US: first 1–2 days diminished/impaired flow on color Doppler; later "Medusa head" of dilated collateral arteries (Ch.28, p.527, Fig. 28.5).
- HLA-B*08 + DRB1*03 found in 16% of 211 patients with late-onset inflammation (p.525). Hyaluronidase allergy incidence 1:200,014 (p.525).
- Erasmus referral centre sees a mean of 2 vascular adverse events/month (p.526).

### Hyaluronidase dosing (Ch.22 p.419; Ch.27 p.520 Table 27.1; Ch.28 p.528)
- US-guided injection allows "a lower dose (35–50 iu) in comparison with the high dose (500 iu>) usually used in non-guided procedures" (p.419).
- Table 27.1 HA degradation resistance (p.520):
  - Less resistant: Restylane Lyft, Restylane L → ~2.5 U hyaluronidase / 0.2 mL gel in vivo
  - Moderately resistant: Belotero, Juvederm Ultra, Restylane Silk, Restylane, Restylane Defyne, Juvederm Volbella → 5–10 U / 0.2 mL
  - More resistant: Restylane Ultra Plus, Restylane Refyne, Revanesse Versa, Juvederm Vollure, Juvederm Voluma → initial ≥20 U / 0.2 mL, multiple injections may be needed
  - Monophasic fillers less responsive than biphasic (p.520).
- Late inflammatory nodules: ciprofloxacin + clarithromycin 3–6 weeks; hyaluronidase 48 h–2 weeks after antibiotics start, 30–300 units per nodule (p.520).
- Vascular occlusion (Ch.28): ~80 patients over 3 years with impending necrosis treated US-guided, never >150 units total, no scarring unless referred >4 days (p.528); vs guideline pulsed high-dose ≥500 iu hourly, 3–8 sessions (p.528). Aspirin once daily × 7 days (p.521).
- Needle: 25 G or thicker; in-plane, entry 0.5–2 cm lateral/caudal to filler, angle 30–45° (p.527; CME Q9–10 p.546).
- Filler removal (Ch.25 p.504): PAAG/polyalkylimide aspirated with ≥14 G needle or extruded via No. 11 scalpel under US; PLLA and CaHA extractable (easier, less dense); silicone and PMMA cannot be percutaneously extracted.

### Imaging accuracy / device numbers (for scanner-comparison context)
- Dermatologic US requires ≥15 MHz with color Doppler (p.7, p.415). Axial resolution: 15 MHz = 100 μm; 70 MHz = 30 μm (Ch.1, p.5). 22 MHz probe ≈72 μm resolution, penetrates 8–10 mm (Ch.12, p.260). Fixed-frequency 20–100 MHz devices: penetration 6–7 mm at 20 MHz, 3 mm at 75 MHz; tunable 6–18 MHz defines lesions down to 0.1 mm and reaches 60 mm depth (Ch.4, p.78). Detection threshold for cutaneous lesions at 15 MHz = 0.1 mm (CME Q5, p.531). Small handheld devices have lower sensitivity for tiny filler deposits and slow flow (p.415). Proficiency ≈300 examinations/year (p.416).
- Aesthetic face protocol (Ch.1 p.11): scan whole face + both submandibular regions + lacrimal, parotid, submandibular glands; grayscale then color Doppler, ≥2 perpendicular axes, spectral Doppler.

### Aging / volume-loss numbers
- (no quantitative data in this book) — only qualitative: SLEB in sun-exposed skin; frequent facial wrinkles map (Fig. 6.1, p.101) and muscle→wrinkle mapping (Table 6.1).

### Other aesthetic procedures — US signs (Ch.22 pp.423–429) [for interpreting post-treatment scans]
- Mesotherapy: lobular/mixed panniculitis, granulomas, regional lymphedema.
- Cryolipolysis (−2 to −10 °C): hypodermal hyperechogenicity/heterogeneity, anechoic fat-necrosis foci.
- Radiofrequency: dermal + hypodermal thickening, dermis hypoechoic, hypodermis hyperechoic, septal thickening.
- Threads: PDO, fragment in 2–3 months; dermal fragments → granulomatous tissue (Fig. 22.13, right cheek 2 months post-PDO).
- Liposuction: hyperechoic hypodermis lacking lobules, serohematic collections, late laminar fibrous bands.
- Blepharoplasty: dermal/muscle hypoechogenicity, fat-pad hyperechogenicity, serohematic collections, suture fragments; late orbicularis thinning.
- Rhinoplasty: inflammatory/granulomatous tissue, suture fragments, cartilage grafts (hypoechoic bands), polyethylene (hyperechoic bands), nasal-bone irregularities; may be combined with filler rhinomodulation.
- Microfocused ultrasound (MFU) can be targeted to a chosen layer under US (Fig. 27.7, p.523).

## Assessment
- Quantitative content is THIN for the purposes of a per-region rule set. The only hard layer number is "facial dermis ≈1.0–1.5 mm" (p.417) and the qualitative statement that facial dermis is thinner than trunk dermis and that hypodermal fat is absent in eyelids and lips and fibroadipose (non-lobular) on the nose. There are no per-site skin/fat/SMAS thickness tables, no vessel depths in mm, no injection volumes, no G' values, no longevity curves, and no aging volume-loss data.
- What IS uniquely useful: (1) authoritative statement that fillers land in the HYPODERMIS, not dermis, because of a 1.0–1.5 mm dermis vs 1.0–1.5 cm needles; (2) per-filler persistence: cross-linked HA up to 7 years detectable, non-cross-linked ~6 months, PAAG unchanged ≥18 months, silicone oil/PMMA/pure silicone never shrink, PCL matrix shrinks but spots persist; (3) HA pocket geometry vs plane — oval on periosteum (zygoma, chin) and in fat pads, meandering between muscle fibres when deep to masseter, stable "for many months" in deep fat pads where muscle movement is minimal; (4) migration rules — silicone oil by gravity/dynamics across regions, PAAG caudally from malar to commissure after years, HA through cannula tracks/fascia punctures and intramuscular injection, any filler to submandibular lymph nodes; (5) hyaluronidase dose table by product (2.5 / 5–10 / ≥20 U per 0.2 mL) and US-guided doses (35–50 iu; ≤150 iu total for vascular events); (6) danger-zone list per region with the glabella flagged as highest-risk, and lip arterial variants (superficial facial-artery loop to upper hypodermis/dermis–hypodermis border; caliber-persistent dermal artery); (7) a few real deposit dimensions (nasal tip HA 1.93 cm; glabella/nasolabial/lip silicone 2.5–4.5 cm long × 0.5–0.9 cm thick).
- Regions covered best: lips/perioral (vascular anatomy + variants), glabella (risk), midface/masseter/chin (HA pocket behaviour), nose (layer description), eyelids (layer stack, migration mechanism). Regions with essentially nothing: temple, brow, jawline, neck.
- For the scanner project, use this book to justify: modelling filler as a subcutaneous (not dermal) volume; region-dependent persistence (deep fat pads retain volume longest); and expecting the visible surface effect to decay before the US-detectable mass does ("clinical effect wears off some time before the HA mass is completely cleared", p.526).
